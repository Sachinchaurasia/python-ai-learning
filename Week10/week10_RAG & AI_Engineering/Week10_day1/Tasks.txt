Q1. Why can't we simply create one embedding for an entire 100-page PDF?

An embedding converts text into one vector representing the meaning of that text.

If we embed an entire 100-page PDF as one vector:

100-page PDF
     ↓
   Embedding
     ↓
One huge vector

we lose the ability to precisely identify which part of the document contains the relevant information.

For example, suppose your proposal contains:

Page 5  → Research objectives
Page 20 → Methodology
Page 45 → Expected outcomes
Page 80 → References

A researcher asks:

"Which proposal discusses deep-learning-based image classification?"

If the entire 100-page document has one embedding, retrieval can only say:

This document is relevant.

It cannot easily identify:

Page 23, paragraph 2 → relevant information

Instead, we do:

100-page PDF
      ↓
Extract text
      ↓
Clean text
      ↓
Split into chunks
      ↓
Chunk 1 → embedding
Chunk 2 → embedding
Chunk 3 → embedding
...
Chunk 200 → embedding

Then retrieval can find the specific relevant chunks.

This is especially important for your system:

Research Proposal
       ↓
Chunks
       ↓
Embeddings
       ↓
Vector Search
       ↓
Relevant proposal sections
       ↓
Compare with referee expertise
Q2. What is a chunk?

A chunk is a smaller meaningful piece of a document.

For example:

Original document
──────────────────────────────

Artificial intelligence is...
Machine learning is...
Deep learning is...
Transformers are...
Natural language processing is...

We can divide it into:

Chunk 1
Artificial intelligence is...
Machine learning is...

Chunk 2
Deep learning is...
Transformers are...

Chunk 3
Natural language processing is...

Each chunk can then receive its own embedding.

Think of:

Document
   ↓
Paragraphs / sentences
   ↓
Chunks
   ↓
Embeddings

A chunk is therefore the unit of text that your retrieval system searches.

Q3. What happens if chunks are too large?

Suppose we create:

Chunk = 5,000 words

The chunk contains many different topics:

AI
Machine Learning
Deep Learning
Dataset
Methodology
Results
References

Its embedding represents a mixture of many concepts.

That makes retrieval less precise.

For example:

User query:
"Deep learning methodology"

The system may retrieve a huge chunk containing:

AI + ML + DL + dataset + results + references

instead of a focused section.

Problems with very large chunks
Less precise retrieval
More unrelated information
More tokens sent to the LLM
Higher processing cost
Harder for the LLM to identify the exact relevant information
Q4. What happens if chunks are too small?

Now imagine:

Chunk 1 → "The proposed"
Chunk 2 → "method uses"
Chunk 3 → "deep learning"
Chunk 4 → "for classification."

Each chunk contains very little meaning.

The embedding:

"The proposed"

doesn't tell us much.

Problems with very small chunks
Not enough context
Weak semantic meaning
Retrieval becomes fragmented
Important relationships between sentences can be lost
More chunks → more embeddings → more storage/search work

So we want a balance:

Too large
    ↓
Low precision

Too small
    ↓
Low context

Good chunk
    ↓
Enough context + good retrieval
Q5. What is chunk overlap?

Chunk overlap means deliberately repeating some text between neighboring chunks.

Suppose we have:

S1
S2
S3
S4
S5
S6

with:

sentences_per_chunk = 3
overlap_sentences = 1

we get:

Chunk 0
S1 S2 S3

Chunk 1
    S3 S4 S5

Chunk 2
        S5 S6

Notice:

S3

appears in both Chunk 0 and Chunk 1.

And:

S5

appears in both Chunk 1 and Chunk 2.

That's chunk overlap.

Q6. Why does overlap help preserve context?

Imagine a document says:

The researchers collected 10,000 images.

These images were then divided into training and testing datasets.

Suppose the chunk boundary happens here:

Chunk 1:
The researchers collected 10,000 images.

Chunk 2:
These images were then divided into training and testing datasets.

The second chunk contains:

"These images..."

But what images?

The previous sentence explains it.

With overlap:

Chunk 1:
The researchers collected 10,000 images.

Chunk 2:
The researchers collected 10,000 images.
These images were then divided into training and testing datasets.

Now Chunk 2 retains the context.

So overlap helps prevent information from being lost at chunk boundaries.

Q7. Character-based vs Sentence-based chunking
Character-based chunking

We split according to the number of characters.

Example:

chunk_size = 500

So:

Characters 1–500 → Chunk 1
Characters 501–1000 → Chunk 2
Characters 1001–1500 → Chunk 3

The problem is that we might cut a sentence:

The researchers developed a new deep learning

and then:

model for identifying...

That's not ideal semantically.

Character-based
Text
 ↓
Count characters
 ↓
Split every N characters

Advantage: simple and predictable size.

Disadvantage: may break sentences or words.

Sentence-based chunking

This is what you're currently implementing.

First:

sentences = split_sentences(text)

Then:

Sentence 1
Sentence 2
Sentence 3
Sentence 4
Sentence 5

and create:

Chunk 1 → S1 S2 S3
Chunk 2 → S3 S4 S5
Advantage

Chunks generally preserve complete thoughts and are more semantically meaningful.

Disadvantage

Sentence lengths aren't equal.

For example:

Sentence A = 20 words
Sentence B = 25 words
Sentence C = 150 words

A three-sentence chunk could therefore become quite large.

Quick comparison
Feature	Character-based	Sentence-based
Splitting unit	Characters	Sentences
Simple	✅	✅
Preserves sentences	❌	✅
Predictable size	✅	Less predictable
Semantic quality	Moderate	Generally better
Your current implementation	❌	✅
Q8. Why is metadata important in RAG?

Metadata gives us information about where a chunk came from and what it represents.

Your chunk currently looks like:

{
    "chunk_id": 0,
    "source": "research_document.txt",
    "document_type": "research",
    "text": "..."
}

The embedding represents the meaning.

Metadata tells us the identity and context.

Think:

Embedding
    ↓
"What does this text mean?"

Metadata
    ↓
"Where did this text come from?"

Metadata is extremely useful when retrieving results.

For example:

Query
 ↓
Vector Search
 ↓
Top 5 chunks
 ↓
Metadata tells us:

source = proposal_101.pdf
page = 23
section = methodology
document_type = research_proposal

Then your application can show:

Relevant information found in Proposal 101, Methodology section, page 23.

🧠 The key idea to remember

For your Week 10 learning, remember this chain:

DOCUMENT
   ↓
CLEAN
   ↓
CHUNK
   ↓
METADATA
   ↓
EMBEDDING
   ↓
VECTOR DATABASE
   ↓
RETRIEVAL
   ↓
LLM

And the three most important concepts are:

Chunk       = What piece of text do we search?

Embedding   = What does that piece of text mean?

Metadata    = Where/what does that piece of text belong to?

You have now understood the core chunking concepts needed before moving into advanced embeddings and vector storage.
